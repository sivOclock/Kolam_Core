Lesson 0 · Transformers, from zero

Assume you know nothing. This page is the whole idea, slowly: tokens, vectors, attention, masks, encoders and decoders. Everything else in the course zooms into one box of it.

0 · the whole idea (this page) → 1 · the loop in our model → 2 · attention by hand → 3–7 · the pieces, one by one

The ten-second version

A transformer is a machine that takes a list of things — words, sounds, image patches, our kolam digits — turns each into a vector, and then lets the vectors look at each other and mix, over and over, until each one “knows enough” to answer a question.

That question is almost always: “given everything so far, what comes next?”

ChatGPT, Claude, BERT, the image models that caption photos — all the same machine with different clothes. Both of our kolam models too: Model A wraps a transformer around an image and writes the encoding digit by digit; Model B wraps one around the image and reads all the gaps at once. If you understand this page, you understand 90% of both.

1 · Everything first becomes numbers: a vector

Computers can't multiply words. So step one, always: cut the input into tokens and give every token a vector — a list of numbers. Think of the list as coordinates: a point somewhere in a huge space. Similar meanings end up at nearby points, because training pushes them there.

Each token is replaced by a swatch row (here 6 numbers instead of the real 256). Look how cat and dog — similar things — get nearly the same swatches, while mat and run get different ones:

dog
mat

Why a machine would ever mix things: a lone vector can only say what one token is. To understand a sentence — or an image — a token has to know about the others. That mixing is attention.

Our model's tokens: "0"…"8", "|", <end>. Each one got its own learned vector. That vocabulary is tiny — an LLM's is tens of thousands.

2 · Attention is a soft lookup

Forget neural networks for a second. Imagine a library. You walk in with a question. Every book has a short label. You compare your question to every label and get a match score. Then — this is the only unusual part — you don't pick one book. You take a little from every book, in proportion to how well it matched. Mostly the best matches, a pinch of everything else. That mix is your answer.

your question is a Query
“something animal-like that purrs”

you take these shares of each book's Value (its content):
62% of cat + 24% of dog + 9% of car + 5% of mat
≈ “an animal, mostly cat-ish”.

In a transformer, the “buttons” on the machine are these three:

query = what am I looking for?  ·  key = what do I offer?  ·  value = what do I hand over?

Each meaning is itself a vector, so “comparing” two of them means a dot product: multiply the numbers pairwise and add — big when the vectors point the same way. Big score = good match. Then softmax turns the raw scores into percentages that sum to 100, and the output is the percentage-weighted mix of the values. In symbols — the only formula on this page, promise:

weights = softmax( Q · Kᵀ / √d )   then   output = weights · V

We'll actually compute this with real numbers in Lesson 2 — pocket calculator style.

3 · Self-attention — everyone looks at everyone

Here's the trick that makes the whole thing tick: use the same list on both sides. Each token makes its own query, and every token in the list offers its key and value. The sentence attends to itself. Click a word below to make it the query and watch where its attention goes.

who is allowed to look at whom: rows ask, columns answer

4 · Masked attention — the “must not cheat” rule

There are two ways to use self-attention, and the difference is one tiny rule: is looking ahead allowed?

Masked self-attention (the GPT mode above) blocks every token from seeing anything to its right — the future simply isn't there yet. When the model writes text word by word, it must decide each next word from the past alone; a mask enforces exactly that, always. Without it, the model could peek at the answer it's supposed to produce. With it, one training pass can check every position's prediction at once (that's Lesson 5's magic trick).

Our Model A writes encodings, so its self-attention is masked, exactly like GPT's. Lesson 1 replays exactly this masked loop on a real kolam, token by token.

5 · Many heads, one block, N layers

One way of looking is not enough. So the vector (256 numbers wide, in our model) is split into heads — in ours, 8 of 32 numbers — each with its own Q, K, V. One head might learn “track the subject”, another “track the previous word”, another “look at the punctuation”. Then the 8 results are glued back together. A full transformer layer has just two mixing steps, wrapped in safety rails:

  • Attention mixes across tokens — information travels between positions.
  • MLP thinks within one token — each vector is processed alone, 256→1024→256.
  • Residual (x + …): every sublayer keeps a copy of its input on a side path, so nothing is ever overwritten and gradients can flow in deep stacks.
  • LayerNorm: rescales each vector after every step, so the numbers never drift out of range.
  • That whole drawing is one layer. Stack it: ours is 4 layers deep, GPT-3 was 96. Same block, different height.

6 · Positions — without them, it's a bag of words

Attention compares vectors, and vectors only know what a token is, not where it sat. Shuffle the input and each word's mix looks the same as before. That's fatal: “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” would give the model the exact same picture. So we add position information to every token's vector. Toggle it below:

GPT adds “I am token #17”. Our encoder adds “I am row 3, column 5 of the encoding” — same idea, shaped to the task. That's Lesson 4.

7 · Three bodies: encoder, decoder, and both

You now know the only two ingredients: attention and MLP, wrapped in residuals and norms. Everything famous is a different way of arranging them:

Encoder only

BERT, image models (ViT) — and our Model B. Reads and understands; doesn't write.

Decoder only

GPT, Claude, LLMs — and our Model A. Writes, one token at a time, never looking ahead.

Encoder + decoder

The 2017 translation paper. One transformer reads one thing (a sentence — for us, the image through the CNN “eye”), the other writes another, glancing over through cross-attention.

8 · How it learns: guess the next token, a billion times

Training is one game, played on mountains of text (or kolam encodings): cover the next token, guess it from everything before, compare with the truth, nudge every weight a tiny bit toward less surprise. That's it. Cross-entropy measures the surprise; gradient descent does the nudging.

Nobody tells the model grammar, or masks, or where rows end. Those are habits that fall out of the game — including, for our model, learning that "|" ends a row and <end> means stop.

Now you know the words

token
one chunk of input: a word, or for us one encoding digit like “6” or “|”.
vector
a list of numbers; a point in space; similar meanings → nearby points.
embedding
the vector a token is turned into (learned during training).
attention
each vector searching the others and mixing in what it finds.
query / key / value
what I'm looking for / what I offer / what I hand over.
softmax
turns raw scores into percentages summing to 100.
head
one parallel attention with its own Q/K/V — ours has 8.
mask
blocks a token from seeing the future, so it can't peek at answers.
encoder / decoder
the read-everything half / the write-one-token-at-a-time half.
cross-attention
the decoder's tokens looking over at the encoder's (or the image's).
residual / LayerNorm
“keep the old value around” / “keep the numbers in range”.
logits
the raw scores before softmax — our model makes 11 per step.

Where this leaves us: our Model A is a small decoder (4 layers, masked) that cross-attends to an image and writes encoding digits; Model B is an encoder that reads every gap at once. Lesson 1 puts the theory to work: you watch Model A write a real kolam, token by token.
